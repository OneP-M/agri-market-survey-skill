# Market Survey and Analysis Competition Workflow

Use this skill after the user has identified a concrete topic, product, service, market, region, or target group.

The selected topic is the user's choice. Preserve it throughout the workflow. Do not replace it with a more fashionable topic unless the user explicitly asks for alternatives. You may suggest narrowing the scope when the selected topic cannot be surveyed reliably, but explain the reason before applying the change.

## 1. Lock the topic

Rewrite the user's topic as a precise research statement containing:

- target market or industry;
- product, service, or consumption scene;
- respondent group;
- geographic scope;
- actual market decision to be supported;
- intended users of the findings.

The topic should answer:

> Who is being surveyed, about what product or service, in which market, and what decision will the findings help someone make?

Avoid titles that contain only broad concepts such as digital economy, artificial intelligence, rural revitalization, green development, cultural confidence, or consumption upgrading.

A workable title should be narrow enough to identify:

- a real respondent;
- a real behavior;
- a real market actor;
- a real action that may change after the survey.

## 2. Assess survey feasibility before designing the questionnaire

Check the following before doing substantial research:

- Can the intended respondents be reached through campus, community, online, workplace, platform, or partner channels?
- Can ordinary respondents understand the topic without specialist knowledge?
- Have they actually purchased, used, experienced, searched for, or considered the relevant product or service?
- Can the questionnaire be completed online in approximately 5–8 minutes?
- Can the team obtain enough valid responses within the available time?
- Is there a market actor that can use the result for product, price, channel, service, communication, or user-segmentation decisions?

Do not assume access to professionals, enterprises, farmers, patients, policy staff, or industry practitioners unless the user confirms that access.

When access is uncertain, prefer a consumer-side survey over a production-side or professional-side survey. Do not convert a consumer survey into a technical adoption study merely because the topic has a technical background.

## 3. Check overlap with recent competition topics

Before finalizing the research question, search the official competition announcements, school and provincial winning-work lists, public topic collections, and any materials supplied by the user for the latest three completed competition years.

Create an overlap table containing:

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Existing title | Original published title |
| Product or scene | What market was studied |
| Respondent | Who was surveyed |
| Core question | What behavior or decision was examined |
| Main method | Questionnaire, interview, experiment, segmentation, etc. |
| Overlap judgment | Similar, partially similar, or distinct |
| Response | Exclude, narrow, or retain |

Do not claim novelty merely because the wording is different. A topic should be considered repetitive when it keeps the same:

- product or service;
- respondent group;
- core behavior;
- decision question;
- recommendation logic.

Frequently repeated patterns should receive additional scrutiny, including generic purchase-intention surveys, broad satisfaction surveys, brand-awareness surveys, livestream-commerce studies, green-consumption awareness studies, rural e-commerce status studies, tourism-intention studies, and “current situation plus recommendations” reports.

If the archive is incomplete, state the limitation and distinguish:

- verified non-overlap;
- probable similarity;
- not yet checked.

Novelty may come from a genuinely different market problem, such as:

- post-purchase repurchase loss rather than first-purchase intention;
- actual use of information or traceability tools rather than awareness;
- service recovery and after-sales rather than satisfaction alone;
- acceptance of non-standard, repaired, shared, recycled, or lower-cost products;
- channel switching or platform migration;
- the conflict between social-purpose consumption and repeat commercial demand;
- information transparency, trust, or consumer-rights problems;
- different needs across clearly identified customer segments.

## 4. Build the social-significance argument from authoritative policy

Use policies and official documents from the recent three years when they are relevant. Prefer central government, ministries, statistical authorities, regulators, and official local-government sources.
